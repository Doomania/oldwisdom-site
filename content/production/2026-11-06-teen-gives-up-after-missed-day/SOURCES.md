# Sources and Claim Audit — teen gives up after a missed day

**Research date:** 2026-11-06  
**Status:** `DRAFT_RESEARCHED` — source map, not independent editorial QA.  
**Method boundary:** The one-question, teen-chosen restart is OWR editorial synthesis, **not a tested teen or parent intervention**. An individual response may make room for a restart; it cannot decide whether a teen resumes.

## Sources

1. **Lally, Phillippa; van Jaarsveld, Cornelia H. M.; Potts, Henry W. W.; Wardle, Jane (2010), “How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world.”** *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40, 998–1009. DOI: 10.1002/ejsp.674. Original publisher abstract: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674 ; accessible full-paper mirror inspected with `pdftotext` and by independent QA, used as the reader-facing link: https://storage.googleapis.com/mailerlite-uploads-prod/1931422/6eeOk9svV7aeAMDHeKomMKhedjFidiHisO7wDdx4.pdf . The institutional repository copy https://repositorio.ispa.pt/bitstream/10400.12/3364/1/IJSP_998-1009.pdf returned a challenge or HTML rather than reader-accessible PDF and is not the article link. Mirror availability may change; recheck at release.  
   **Claim-safe use:** University student volunteers chose one daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour for 12 weeks. Ninety-six enrolled; 82 provided enough data for the main automaticity analysis. The specific omission analysis identified **140 missed opportunities among 55 participants** after three performed occasions; only 67 of those omissions had a next-day score following resumed performance. A single omission did not materially change measured automaticity in that narrow comparison. This is context for challenging the idea that *one miss necessarily destroys a habit*, not evidence that resumption is guaranteed. Article wording should omit the sample arithmetic and link the narrow conclusion inline.  
   **Limit:** Not a teen sample; not a study of parental reactions, perfectionism, multiple missed days, keeping school promises, or the restart script. The omission result is **not** an outcome analysis of all 96 enrollees or all 82 with sufficient main-study data. Automaticity was self-reported, and the comparison conditions required earlier performance and, in the next-day subset, actual resumption; it cannot establish that a teen will resume after a lapse. No universal “never miss twice” rule or fixed habit-formation timeline.

2. **Kenneth R. Ginsburg, MD, FAAP, American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org, “Perfectionism: How to Help Your Child Avoid the Pitfalls.”** https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/young-adult/Pages/What-Fuels-Perfectionism.aspx . Author's automated fetch showed title only; independent reviewer directly inspected the full AAP page and confirmed the mistakes/process wording.  
   **Claim-safe use:** Some young people with perfectionistic concerns may interpret mistakes or criticism as evidence of inadequacy. The clinician recommends realistic expectations and questions about process and what could be changed next time; time, energy and rest are real constraints. Attribute to Ginsburg/AAP in the article beside the claim.  
   **Limit:** Advice, not a controlled trial of the OWR question or proof that every teen who says “I give up” has perfectionism, anxiety or a fixed mindset. Do not claim a single parent's line causes a restart or diagnosis.

3. **National Institute of Mental Health, “Teen Depression: More Than Just Moodiness.”** https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/teen-depression  
   **Claim-safe use:** Weeks of low mood with difficulty concentrating or doing formerly enjoyed things, withdrawal, lost interest, changed sleep/eating or falling grades warrant talking with a trusted adult and asking a doctor about professional help. The page points US readers to 988 for suicide/crisis support. One abandoned plan by itself is not a diagnosis. Use the separate NIMH suicide FAQ below for immediate-danger action and its adjacent link.  
   **Limit:** A teen-facing US clinical information page; not a diagnostic tool for a parent, a rule that every missed practice needs treatment, or evidence for the restart method. Global emergency wording must not imply 988 operates outside the US.

4. **National Institute of Mental Health, “Frequently Asked Questions About Suicide.”** https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/suicide-faq  
   **Claim-safe use:** Immediate life-threatening danger calls for emergency services (911 in the US); suicidal thoughts or crisis call for immediate help and US 988 support. In global copy, direct readers to their local emergency or crisis service without implying that US numbers work everywhere.  
   **Limit:** This is a safety route for urgent risk, not an inference from an ordinary missed day. Link directly beside the urgent action; the teen-depression page covers the persistent-symptom lane separately.

5. **Old Wisdom // Retold, live primary product page, “The Discipline Playbook.”** https://oldwisdomretold.com/books/the-discipline-playbook#sample ; local source `content/books.json`, discipline object.  
   **Claim-safe use:** The page explicitly lists “You missed a day and feel like giving up the whole plan.” The public `#sample` is **Chapter 1 — The Promise Nobody Heard**, which shows how to name one next action and a rough when. A parent can optionally offer that teen-owned first-action tool after the free guide stands alone.  
   **Limit:** Product positioning, not independent evidence of effectiveness. The public excerpt does **not** contain a missed-day-specific chapter; never label it a missed-day excerpt or claim the book was tested for restart outcomes.

## Claim-to-article controls and portfolio boundaries

- “A missed chance need not erase prior practice”: Lally, limited adult automaticity comparison; no “proven restart” claim. Link within the same paragraph.
- “Some teens read imperfection as inadequacy; ask about process”: Ginsburg/AAP, guidance only; no claim about this particular teen's motive or a measured parent-script effect. Link next to the claim.
- “Persistent changes in mood/function or immediate risk need more than a habit plan”: NIMH, linked in each material support/safety paragraph. Ordinary one-off lapse is explicitly separated from symptoms and crisis.
- “What, when, if barrier” and any wording about the parent's reaction: labelled editorial household suggestion. Parent retains duty, safety and school authority; do not erase a missed obligation to someone else. [Reliability-signal](/articles/reliability-signal) covers that separate repair job.
- Search phrases in the card are unverified planning hypotheses, not measured search volume, quoted parents, or Reddit prevalence. Examples of sport practice, game streak and household duty are illustrations, not observed cases.
- Separate from `/articles/stop-reminding-homework` (daily tracking), `/articles/task-start-runway` (before a task starts), and `/articles/phone-takes-whole-evening` (designing an evening). No implied efficacy from internal pages.
