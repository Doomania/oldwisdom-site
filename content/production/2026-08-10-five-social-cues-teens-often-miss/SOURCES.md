## Brief claim audit

| Content claim used in batch | Support | Wording guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Emotion recognition from faces and voices continues developing through childhood and adolescence. | Mancini et al.; Morningstar et al. | Presented as a developmental pattern, not as proof that any one teen's social difficulty is caused by delayed development. |
| Eye gaze and body orientation are foundational parts of nonverbal social communication and help regulate attention or interaction. | Conty et al.; Dalmaso et al. | Used to justify noticing where attention is aimed. The batch does not claim a specific gaze pattern can "prove" acceptance or rejection. |
| Vocal tone carries social-emotional information that can matter for adjustment, so hearing the sentence alone is not enough. | Morningstar et al.; Pichon et al. | Framed as support for noticing tone shifts, not as a rule that teens must decode every voice perfectly. |
| Text-based communication removes many nonverbal cues, which is one reason adolescents use emoji to carry tone and emotion. | George et al. | Used specifically for text ambiguity, not to claim all digital communication is harmful or inferior. |
| In one ecological study of girls aged 11-13, negative peer interactions on social media were associated with more sustained negative affect than negative in-person interactions. | Hamilton et al. | The batch scopes this carefully to one study population and avoids broad claims that online interaction is always worse. |
| Maintained contact, relationship maintenance behaviors, and reopened interaction matter for closeness or social adjustment in adolescence. | Ferguson et al.; Rousseau et al. | Turned into the practical "warmth versus reciprocity" distinction as an editorial coaching framework, not a direct research term. |
| The five-cue scan, the "warmth is not the same as reciprocity" distinction, and the parent debrief questions are editorial frameworks built from the source set above. | Inference from the combined evidence. | Explicitly treated as practical teaching tools, not direct quoted models from the literature. |

## Claims intentionally avoided

- No clinical claims about anxiety disorders, autism, ADHD, trauma, depression, or any other diagnosis.
- No claim that missing cues is the only reason teens struggle socially.
- No promise that reading these five cues will produce friendship, popularity, or confidence.
- No claim that one text reply, facial look, or tone shift reveals certain intent.
- No long quotations from copyrighted sources.
