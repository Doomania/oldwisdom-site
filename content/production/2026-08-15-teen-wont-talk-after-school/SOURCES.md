# Sources and Claim Audit

Research date: 2026-08-15
Audience: PARENT
Status: publication approved 2026-08-15

## Authoritative and research sources

1. American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren, “Communication Dos and Don’ts.”
   https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/Communication-Dos-and-Donts.aspx

   **Access and claim-safe use:** Direct extraction exposed only the page title; current search-index wording was inspected and states that a young person who comes home tired may benefit from rest or a snack before a parent raises what is on their mind, and that timing/place matter. Used as practical timing guidance only. It does not establish a universal decompression interval or explain one teen’s silence.

2. Zapf et al., “A systematic review of the association between parent-child communication and adolescent mental health,” JCPP Advances, 2023/2024.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11143954/

   **Claim-safe use:** Thirty-seven studies were included; adolescent-rated communication quality was associated with mental-health measures, with important design and measurement limits. Most studies were cross-sectional. Used to establish relevance and limits—not to claim that a script or routine prevents mental-health problems.

3. Baudat et al., “How Do Adolescents Manage Information in the Relationship with Their Parents?” Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 2022.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9090863/

   **Claim-safe use:** In 332 Swiss adolescents, more communicative patterns co-occurred with higher perceived parental involvement, autonomy support, and autonomous reasons for disclosure. The person-centred observational design does not prove that one parent behaviour causes disclosure, and it does not generalise every finding to every family.

4. Vijayakumar and Pfeifer, “Self-disclosure during adolescence: exploring the means, targets, and types of personal exchanges,” Current Opinion in Psychology, 2020.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7130455/

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports the broad developmental boundary that adolescents increasingly manage self-disclosure across parents and peers while parents can remain important sources of support. It does not support mind-reading, forced disclosure, or a claim that reduced parent disclosure is harmless in every case.

5. National Institute of Mental Health, “Children and Mental Health: Is This Just a Stage?” revised 2024.
   https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/children-and-mental-health

   **Claim-safe use:** Directly inspected. Supports seeking help when behaviour or emotions last for weeks, cause distress, or interfere at home, school, or with friends; supports immediate help for unsafe behaviour or talk of harming self or others. US crisis numbers are not reproduced; public copy gives globally applicable local emergency/crisis wording.

## Claim-level map

| Published guidance | Support | Boundary preserved |
|---|---|---|
| Timing and place can make a conversation easier; a tired teen may need food/rest before a parent raises a topic. | HealthyChildren indexed guidance | No magic interval, diagnosis, or guarantee of later disclosure. |
| Adolescents actively manage disclosure as privacy and autonomy develop. | Baudat et al.; Vijayakumar and Pfeifer | Privacy is not treated as proof of safety, honesty, or danger. |
| Warmth/involvement, autonomy support, and structure can coexist. | Baudat et al. | Association, not a causal promise; parent safety authority remains intact. |
| Communication quality is relevant but cannot be reduced to one script. | Zapf et al. | Mostly cross-sectional evidence; no prevention or treatment claim. |
| Sustained change, distress, functional interference, unsafe behaviour, or harm talk requires closer action. | NIMH | Quietness itself is not pathologised; immediate risk bypasses the routine. |
| RESET → NOTICE → INVITE → RETURN | OWR editorial synthesis | Not a clinical tool, diagnostic test, treatment, or guaranteed disclosure method. |

## Portfolio distinctness

- `quiet-kids-and-confidence`: personality/response speed is not confidence. This article does not assess confidence.
- `quiet-or-lonely-teen`: distinguishes preferred solitude from unwanted disconnection. This article does not decide whether a teen is lonely.
- `why-is-my-teen-avoiding-me`: addresses broader relational distance, touch/public avoidance, repair, and selective fear. This article is limited to the home-arrival transition.
- `read-teen-behaviour-without-guessing`: broad behaviour-interpretation framework. This article provides a narrow after-school routine and scripts.
- `help-teen-start-conversation-without-making-it-weird`: coaches the teen’s peer-conversation skill. This article coaches the parent’s timing and opening at home.

## Claims intentionally avoided

- No claim that every quiet arrival is overload, hunger, anxiety, secrecy, disrespect, or ordinary adolescence.
- No universal “20-minute rule.”
- No promise that lower pressure makes a teen disclose.
- No diagnosis from one-word answers, a closed door, or preferring text.
- No instruction to surrender safety checks, household expectations, or proportionate supervision.
- No implication that a snack, car ride, or script treats a mental-health condition.
