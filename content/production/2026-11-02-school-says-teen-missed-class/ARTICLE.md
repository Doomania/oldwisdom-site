# The School Says Your Teen Missed Class. They Left Home This Morning. What Now?

The attendance alert lands at 10:14: your teenager is absent from second period. You saw them leave with their bag. Your first thought may be *they skipped*—or *the school got it wrong*. Neither tells you where they are now.

**First, find out where your teen is and whether they are safe. Then sort out the register.** Do not wait for the school-day story or start with a punishment. Leaving home is not proof of arriving in class; one alert is not proof of deliberate skipping.

## In the next few minutes: locate, don't accuse

1. **Call the school's attendance office or main office.** Say: “I got an absence alert for second period. I saw my teen leave home this morning. Can you check whether they are on campus, whether they were marked present in another period, and the last confirmed sighting? Please call me back as soon as someone has checked.” Give your callback number. If the school confirms they are safe in another class, you can move to the record check below.
2. **Contact your teen directly.** Try a call and a short text: “School says you missed second period. I need to know where you are and that you're safe. Please call or text now.” Use the transport and family contacts you would normally use if the first checks do not locate them. Ask the school who is coordinating its on-site check; do not assume someone else is doing it.
3. **If their whereabouts remain unknown, act on the risk—not a fixed waiting period.** If you believe your child is missing, contact local police immediately; if there is immediate danger, use local emergency services. Give the last known time and place, clothing, route and any urgent risk information. The [National Center for Missing & Exploited Children says to act immediately when a child is believed missing](https://us.missingkids.org/MissingChild); its follow-up hotline is US-specific. An ordinary attendance-entry error is not itself a missing-child case, but a confirmed safe location must come before arguing about the code.

If your teen replies that they are somewhere else, ask for their exact location, who they are with, and how you can confirm they are safe and get them to a safe place. A reply that does not establish safety is not the end of the check. Keep the school informed if they are not on campus.

## Once they are safe, reconcile what the alert actually means

An alert may refer to a single class, a whole day, or an entry not yet checked. Ask the office for the **date, period, time of the mark, and who can verify it**. Was your teen marked present elsewhere? Was there a late arrival, timetable change, school activity, nurse visit, or sign-in that has not reached the attendance record? Was the class actually missed? Ask whom to contact for a correction, what documentation the school needs, and when you can expect the record to be updated. Keep the alert and your notes until it is resolved.

This is a practical checking list, not a claim that every school uses the same system. The [US Institute of Education Sciences guide to attendance texts](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/guide/how-text-message-parents-reduce-chronic-absence-using-evidence-based-approach) is written for districts and draws on elementary-school research, not on investigations of individual teenagers. It is not evidence that this particular alert is right or wrong. Ask your school how it records and corrects absences; an alert deserves checking, not automatic dismissal.

Once your teen is located, the fact-before-reaction move echoes Chapter 1, “Don't Fight Blind,” in [*The War Playbook*](https://a.co/d/0gu0a2CR). It is a conversation tool, **not** a reason to delay a safety check.

A short message can keep the paper trail calm:

> “Thank you for checking. Please confirm whether the absence was for second period only, the last verified location, and whether the record needs correcting. If the class was missed, please tell me whom my teen should speak to about catching up.”

## Then ask what happened—without supplying the answer

When your teen is safe and you have the school's facts, speak privately. Try:

> “I was worried because I knew you left home, but the school said you weren't in class. I know you are safe now. Walk me through the time between getting there and second period.”

Let them finish. Then ask one useful follow-up: “What made it hard to go into that class?” or “What does the school have wrong?” The [American Academy of Pediatrics advises listening without an alarmed reaction or lecture](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/How-to-Communicate-with-a-Teenager.aspx). Listening is not an agreement to excuse a missed class; it helps you find the right problem before choosing a response.

If your teen later wants help hearing a teacher's account and explaining their own, Chapter 7, “The Listener's High Ground,” in [*The Social Playbook*](https://a.co/d/0gjqlkB6) practises listening and one follow-up question. This is their skill to use, not a trick to extract every school story.

Possible explanations include an attendance mistake, a transport delay, illness, a conflict, fear of a person or class, or an intentional detour. These are questions to check, not diagnoses or a menu for your teen to pick from. If they describe bullying, a threat, or feeling unsafe at school, take that report seriously and ask the school for a safe point of contact and a concrete safety response rather than arranging a confrontation. The [AAP's school-avoidance page](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/health-issues/conditions/emotional-problems/pages/school-avoidance.aspx), adapted from guidance for younger children, names peer problems and threats as possible barriers and suggests involving school staff. Those are prompts to check, not an explanation for this teen. **Do not send them straight back into a situation they say is unsafe without checking it.**

## Make the next school-day step specific

If it was a clerical error, ask the school to correct it and show your teen the updated record. If a class really was missed, agree who will contact the teacher and when: for example, “I'll email the attendance office today; you'll ask the teacher before last period what you missed.” Check the answer together later. You still own safety, transport, and school coordination; your teen can own a manageable part of the repair when it is safe for them to do so. One plan will not cure school avoidance or settle a threat.

For an ordinary, safe catch-up task, Chapter 1, “The Promise Nobody Heard,” in [*The Discipline Playbook*](https://a.co/d/06SKeoFm) turns a vague intention into one visible action and a rough time. It is not a response to danger, bullying or a health concern.

If missed classes recur, or your teen is distressed or having trouble functioning at school or home, bring the pattern—not a label—to the school counselor or nurse and a health professional. The [AAP's younger-child school-avoidance guidance describes coordinating with school staff and a doctor for persistent difficulties](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/health-issues/conditions/emotional-problems/pages/school-avoidance.aspx); [NIMH advises contacting a health professional when symptoms last weeks or months and interfere with daily life](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/children-and-mental-health). You need not wait weeks to address a concrete safety concern or arrange school help for a growing pattern.

If your teen says they intend to kill themselves, do not leave them alone when it is safe to stay, and seek urgent local crisis or emergency help; for immediate life-threatening danger, call local emergency services ([NIMH suicide guidance](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/suicide-faq)). An isolated missed class is not a suicide warning by itself.

## What to do today

Confirm your teen's location, get the exact attendance facts, listen to their account, and name one school contact and one next check-in time. **Optional next step:** if your teen wants to practise checking facts before a charged reply, [explore Chapter 1 of *The War Playbook*](https://a.co/d/0gu0a2CR). No purchase is needed to take the steps above.
