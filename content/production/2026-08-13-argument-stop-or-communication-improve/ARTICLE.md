# Do You Want the Argument to Stop—or Communication to Improve?

You ask your teenager to put their phone downstairs.

They say, “I’m finishing something.”

You explain the rule. They explain why tonight is different. You explain the rule again, this time with more history and more volume. They argue harder—or go completely silent.

At last, the house is quiet.

But what does the quiet mean? Perhaps they understood. Perhaps they are complying while still disagreeing. Perhaps they gave up, shut you out, or are waiting to restart the same argument tomorrow.

Wanting an argument to stop is reasonable. Parents need to keep people safe, make decisions, and prevent every instruction becoming a two-hour debate. But quiet and communication are not always the same result.

The goal is not endless discussion. It is a teenager who can state a view without attack, hear a limit without pretending to agree, and carry the next responsibility. You can stop escalation now **and** practise that capability later.

## Think of quiet as a closed laptop

Closing a laptop stops the noise and clears the table. It does not tell you whether the work was saved.

Ending an argument is similar. Sometimes closing it is exactly right. People may be too angry, frightened, tired, or overwhelmed to continue usefully. Yet once things are calm, you may still need to check whether the important part was saved:

- Does each person know the actual issue?
- Can your teenager explain what they disagree with without attacking anyone?
- Can they show that they heard the limit?
- Is it clear who must do what next?

Research on parent–teen conflict does not say that all conflict is good. Studies distinguish ordinary disagreement from hostility, and much of the evidence is observational, so it cannot prove that one kind of conversation causes a particular outcome. It does suggest that disagreement, hostility, problem-solving, and withdrawal are not interchangeable, and that conflict styles change during adolescence ([Weymouth et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jftr.12126); [Van Doorn, Branje and Meeus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3003790/)).

For a busy parent, the practical point is simpler: do not judge success by volume alone.

## Stop the exchange when stopping is the job

You do not need to complete a communication exercise in the middle of yelling, intimidation, or danger.

You can say:

> “I’m ending this conversation now. The limit still stands. We will return to the ordinary issue when we can speak safely.”

You may remove car keys, separate people, secure medication or weapons, enforce a consequence, or make the decision that belongs to you. Hearing your teenager does not give them equal authority over safety, law, health, school attendance, or reasonable household rules.

If there is immediate danger, violence, imminent self-harm, or a serious medical emergency, act directly and contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service immediately. Do not wait to use this framework. The [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Calling-911-How-and-When-to-Call-for-Help.aspx) and the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/suicide) provide emergency guidance. Ordinary disagreement, one rude reply, an eye roll, or a teen asking for time is not by itself an emergency or a diagnosis.

Once immediate escalation has ended, choose **one ordinary issue**. That is where VOICE → HEAR → CARRY can help.

## VOICE: one clean point each

VOICE means each person states one position. Not five points. Not a character judgment. Not every similar incident since primary school.

Try:

> Parent: “The phone stays outside your bedroom tonight because the agreed cutoff was missed.”

> Teen: “I think tonight was different because I was finishing a group assignment.”

The teenager gets a voice, not a veto. You may keep the rule exactly as stated.

Compare that exchange with this:

> “You always find an excuse. You care more about your phone than this family, and this is why I can’t trust you.”

Now the teen has to defend their whole character before addressing the phone. One clean point keeps the issue small enough to carry.

If your teen adds three complaints, calmly narrow it:

> “I have heard that you also want to discuss weekend screen time. Tonight we are only dealing with the missed cutoff.”

VOICE is not permission for insults, threats, contempt, repeated interruption, or debate without an end. It is a bounded chance to put the real disagreement on the table.

## HEAR: show the point before answering it

HEAR means giving a fair one-sentence account of what the other person is saying.

> Parent: “You are not saying there should be no cutoff. You are saying school group work changed tonight’s situation.”

> Teen: “You are saying the existing agreement applies unless we change it beforehand.”

Accuracy is the target. Agreement is not.

This is like reading back an address before a delivery. You are not saying you chose the destination; you are checking that you heard it correctly.

A parent can understand the reason and still say no:

> “Yes, I understand why you think tonight should be an exception. I am still keeping the phone downstairs.”

A teen can understand the rule and still dislike it:

> “I hear that the cutoff still applies. I do not think it was fair tonight.”

That is communication. It is not surrender, equal authority, or forced agreement.

Parents and adolescents can give different accounts of the same conflict; research cannot tell you automatically whose account is correct in your kitchen ([Ehrlich et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28581657/)). A fair summary simply checks whether each view has been heard before the decision and next action are made.

If “Tell me what you heard” sounds like a test, make it more natural:

> “What part of my point do you think matters most?”

Then answer the same question about theirs.

## CARRY: name the owner of the next action

Many arguments end with a ruling but no owner. Everyone knows who won the last sentence; nobody knows what happens next.

CARRY makes the handover clear:

> “The phone remains downstairs tonight. Tomorrow, you can propose a plan for group assignments near the cutoff. I will listen and decide whether the rule needs an exception.”

The parent carries what belongs to the parent: safety, the boundary, the adult decision, and a proportionate consequence.

The teenager increasingly carries what belongs to them: the task, proposal, choice inside the limit, repair, or learning from a manageable consequence.

### A missed chore

> Parent VOICE: “The bins were your job and they were not put out.”

> Teen HEAR: “You needed me to do it without another reminder.”

> CARRY: “Collection has passed, so bring the full bins back in and set your own reminder for next week.”

### A late lift

> Parent VOICE: “I can collect you at ten, not ten-thirty.”

> Teen HEAR: “You have work early and cannot wait.”

> CARRY: “Choose the ten o’clock lift or arrange another safe plan with an adult we approve. Tell me by eight.”

### A disputed school consequence

> Parent VOICE: “You need to respond to the teacher’s email.”

> Teen HEAR: “You are not asking me to agree with her; you are asking me to deal with it.”

> CARRY: “Write the first draft. I can read it if you want, but you send it.”

The handover is age-appropriate. You do not leave a younger teen to solve an unsafe transport problem or expect a struggling student to manage a complex school meeting alone. Support can stay close while ownership grows.

## Change the channel, not the capability

Not every teenager can manage a polished spoken exchange. A teen may need more processing time, simpler language, a visual list, one question at a time, or help organising thoughts. Disability, development, executive-function needs, learning differences, language needs, and stress can all affect how much they can hold in one conversation.

Use the scaffolding that fits:

- let them write their VOICE point in a text or note;
- offer two possible summaries and ask which is closer;
- draw two boxes labelled “Parent owns” and “Teen owns” for CARRY;
- break the next action into a first step and a check-in;
- include a trusted support person when that helps communication and safety.

A written answer is not a lesser answer. Extra structure is not lower expectations. The capability is stating, hearing, and carrying—not performing a perfect verbal script.

Silence also has more than one possible meaning. It might be a pause, overload, avoidance, fear, refusal, or simply a preference for another channel. One shutdown does not diagnose a teenager or tell you their intent. Keep the required return clear without forcing an answer on the spot:

> “You can answer tonight in writing or talk with me tomorrow at ten. The decision about school attendance is still mine, and we do need to return to it.”

## Try this after the next ordinary argument

Do not reopen every disagreement. Pick one issue that still needs a next step.

1. **VOICE:** “My one point is…” Then give your teen one clean point too.
2. **HEAR:** Each person sums up the other view in one fair sentence.
3. **CARRY:** Name what you own, what your teen owns, and when the next action happens.

Keep it short. If a new issue appears, park it. If the exchange becomes unsafe or hostile again, end it and act on safety or the boundary.

VOICE → HEAR → CARRY is an Old Wisdom Retold practice, not a tested treatment or a promise of fewer arguments. Expert guidance supports calm timing, focused listening, and negotiation when appropriate, while developmental research suggests parental guidance and growing autonomy can coexist ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/teens/communicating-relationships/communicating/conflict-management-with-teens); [Kobak et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5886742/)). No study proves that this three-step practice will change outcomes in your family.

The measure is not “Did my teenager agree with me?”

It is: **Could they state a view, show what they heard, and carry the next part within a real limit?**

Sometimes good parenting ends the argument. Sometimes it returns to the issue. Often it does both—in that order.
