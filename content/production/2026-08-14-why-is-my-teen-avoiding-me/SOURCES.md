## Authoritative sources used

1. American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren, “Stages of Adolescence” (updated 29 April 2024).
   https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/teen/Pages/Stages-of-Adolescence.aspx

   **Authority/scope limit:** General pediatric guidance supports the claim that family relationships and independence change during adolescence while parents remain an important source of support. It cannot explain one teenager’s motive or define a universal amount of “normal” distance.

2. Baudat et al. (2022), “How Do Adolescents Manage Information in the Relationship with Their Parents? A Latent Class Analysis of Disclosure, Keeping Secrets, and Lying.” *Journal of Youth and Adolescence*.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9090863/

   **Design/claim limit:** Cross-sectional self-report study of 332 Swiss adolescents, mean age 15.01. It supports describing increased privacy and information management as possible parts of adolescence and shows that autonomy and connection can coexist. Associations do not prove that parenting caused a disclosure pattern, justify universal prevalence claims, or make ordinary privacy deceptive.

3. Hare, Marston and Allen (2011), “Maternal Acceptance and Adolescents’ Emotional Communication: A Longitudinal Study.”
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3192331/

   **Design/claim limit:** Longitudinal, multi-method study of 184 adolescents and mothers from about age 13 to 16. Teen-perceived maternal acceptance at 13 predicted relative increases in self-reported emotional communication and observed disclosure three years later. It does not prove that one repair script will reopen communication, and its mother-focused, regional US sample limits generalisation. The article uses only the cautious implication that the teenager’s felt experience of acceptance matters, not solely the parent’s intention.

4. Blum et al. (2022), “Adolescent connectedness: cornerstone for health and wellbeing.” *BMJ*.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9600165/

   **Design/claim limit:** Commissioned, externally peer-reviewed evidence overview. It supports the broad claim that family connectedness remains important while peers and online life become more salient. The connectedness literature is broad and largely observational. It does not justify forced closeness, touch, disclosure, or surveillance.

5. Hare et al. (2015), “Undermining Adolescent Autonomy With Parents and Peers: The Enduring Implications of Psychologically Controlling Parenting.”
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715895/

   **Design/claim limit:** Longitudinal multi-method study of 184 adolescents, mothers, and peers from about age 13 to 16. Teen-perceived maternal psychological control predicted relative decreases in displayed autonomy. Psychological control includes manipulative or intrusive pressure and guilt induction. This does not mean every reminder, consequence, supervision decision, or parent mistake is psychological control. Warm structure and legitimate safety boundaries remain compatible with autonomy.

6. Branje et al. (2010), “Longitudinal Associations Between Perceived Parent-Child Relationship Quality and Depressive Symptoms in Adolescence.”
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2902740/

   **Design/claim limit:** Four-wave self-report study of 1,313 Dutch adolescents. Bidirectional associations indicate that depressive symptoms may strain perceived relationship quality and relationship quality may predict later symptoms in some parent/teen groups. It does not identify causation in one family or permit diagnosis of depression from parent avoidance.

7. US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, “Mental Health | Adolescent and School Health” (29 November 2024).
   https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-youth/mental-health/index.html

   **Authority/scope limit:** Public-health guidance supports family and school connectedness as protective and functional change as worth attention. Population-level guidance cannot diagnose a teenager or establish the cause of withdrawal.

8. World Health Organization, “Mental health of adolescents” (1 September 2025).
   https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/adolescent-mental-health

   **Authority/scope limit:** WHO guidance supports noting that anxiety, depression, isolation, and school impairment can coexist with withdrawal. The article does not use population statistics to diagnose one teen. Pattern, duration, impairment, associated changes, and safety signals guide escalation.

9. American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren, “When & How to Talk With Your Child About Sex.”
   https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Talking-to-Your-Young-Child-About-Sex.aspx

   **Authority/scope limit:** Pediatric guidance supports not forcing physical contact and modelling body autonomy by asking before hugs or kisses. The page is broader than adolescence and does not suggest affection is harmful. The article translates it only into consent for ordinary family touch and alternative forms of warmth.

10. Somerville et al. (2013), “Medial prefrontal cortex and the emergence of self-conscious emotion in adolescence.”
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3742683/

    **Design/claim limit:** Experimental behavioural, autonomic and fMRI study of 69 participants aged 8–22.9. Believed peer observation produced stronger self-conscious emotion among adolescents than among children or adults. The small laboratory study cannot explain one teen’s public avoidance, establish a motive, or show that all adolescents are embarrassed by parents.

11. Zapf et al. (2024), “A systematic review of the association between parent-child communication and adolescent mental health.”
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11143954/

    **Design/claim limit:** Systematic review of 37 quantitative studies, mainly cross-sectional and focused on adolescent-rated communication. Communication quality was associated with several mental-health domains, usually with small-to-medium associations. Direction remains unresolved, and the review does not validate THE PORCH LIGHT as prevention or treatment.

12. National Institute of Mental Health, “Frequently Asked Questions About Suicide.”
    https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/suicide-faq

    **Authority/scope limit:** NIMH states that asking directly about suicide does not cause or increase suicidal thoughts and advises not leaving a person alone after stated suicidal intent. The source is US-specific in its service numbers; the article therefore uses globally actionable wording about local emergency or crisis services and does not treat ordinary distance as a suicide warning by itself.

13. World Health Organization, “Responding to children and adolescents who have been sexually abused” (2017).
    https://www.who.int/news/item/19-10-2017-responding-to-children-and-adolescents-who-have-been-sexually-abused

    **Authority/scope limit:** WHO child-centred guidance for health-care responses prioritises safety, privacy, choices, autonomy, wishes, and clear limits of confidentiality. The article applies those principles cautiously when selective fear raises a possible safeguarding concern; it does not claim that selective avoidance proves abuse or substitute a parent script for qualified assessment.

14. World Health Organization, “Child maltreatment” (2026).
    https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/child-maltreatment

    **Authority/scope limit:** WHO defines child maltreatment, notes that it is often hidden, and calls for early recognition plus response and support services. It supports contacting appropriate protection services when serious abuse danger is present, not treating ordinary distance as maltreatment.

15. American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren, “Is It a Medical Emergency, or Not?”
    https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/health-management/Pages/Is-It-a-Medical-Emergency-or-Not.aspx

    **Authority/scope limit:** AAP guidance says a true medical emergency needs immediate emergency treatment. The article generalises the US-specific service wording to local emergency services and does not label ordinary withdrawal as a medical emergency.

## Claims and promises intentionally avoided

- No claim that teen rejection or withdrawal is always normal.
- No claim that public embarrassment means disgust or loss of love.
- No claim that avoiding touch proves abuse, trauma, depression, anxiety, neurodivergence, sensory sensitivity, or any other condition.
- No claim that a parent’s mistake caused the withdrawal.
- No claim that warmth, apology, alternate channels, or THE PORCH LIGHT will restore closeness.
- No universal timeline for distance, repair, or reconnection.
- No claim that respecting privacy removes reasonable supervision, safeguarding, school, health, or household duties.
- No claim that a teenager’s account must be disclosed directly to the adult they fear or avoid.
- No claim that one unanswered safety question proves danger or proves safety.
- No clinical, therapeutic, safeguarding, or treatment label for the OWR practice.
