## Authoritative sources used

1. New Zealand Ministry of Education, “Bullying: help and support for your child,” updated 4 May 2026.
   https://www.education.govt.nz/parents-and-caregivers/schools-year-0-13/health-safety-and-wellbeing/bullying-help-and-support-your-child

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports the distinction between bullying—deliberate harm, a power imbalance and usually repetition—and a one-off argument or fight. Supports school safety responsibility and parent requests for a prevention/response plan. It also identifies assaults, threats and weapons as matters that can move to police. Institutional details are New Zealand-specific and are not presented as universal legal advice.

2. KidsHealth New Zealand / Paediatric Society of New Zealand, “Bullying,” reviewed 22 January 2026.
   https://www.kidshealth.org.nz/bullying

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports calm listening, clarifying facts, agreeing a plan with the child, involving a trusted adult or school, walking away or asking for help, and emergency help where physical threats create a safety concern. It does not prove that one script prevents harm. Country-specific emergency numbers are intentionally omitted.

3. Government of Canada, “Bullying (ages 12 to 17),” page dated 28 May 2026.
   https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/bullying/bullying-ages-12-17.html

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports a loud, firm “stop,” walking or running to safety, avoiding physically fighting back and telling an adult. This is general guidance, not a safety guarantee and not proof that walking away is always possible.

4. US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, “Preventing Youth Violence,” 29 October 2024.
   https://www.cdc.gov/youth-violence/prevention/index.html

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports parent conversations about violence and adult/school responsibility for supportive settings, social-emotional skills and connections to help. Population-level prevention guidance does not validate CLEAR OUT or prove that a phrase prevents an assault.

5. American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren, “Why Bullying Hurts All Children—and What Parents Can Do About It.”
   https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Bullying-Its-Not-Ok.aspx

   **Access and use limit:** The research map verified authoritative metadata/index wording only, including walking away calmly, telling a trusted adult and a non-fighting line. The full article body was not available through direct extraction. It is corroboration only; no verbatim quotation from it appears in the article.

6. StopBullying.gov, “How Youth Can Protect Themselves From Bullying.”
   https://www.stopbullying.gov/resources/research-resources/how-youth-can-protect-themselves

   **Access and use limit:** Robots blocked direct inspection during research. Indexed federal wording corroborates assertive rather than aggressive/fighting/teasing responses and leaving unsafe places. It is not used as intervention-effect proof or quoted verbatim.

7. New Zealand Police, “How to report a crime or incident,” directly inspected 14 August 2026.
   https://www.police.govt.nz/advice-services/how-report-crime-or-incident

   **Claim-safe use:** Supports emergency contact when somebody is badly injured or in danger, there is a serious risk to life, or a crime is being committed and the offenders are still present or have just left. The article uses those criteria for serious injury, trapping/immediate danger and assault in progress. A credible weapon threat is mapped jointly to the serious-risk criterion and the New Zealand Ministry of Education guidance that threats and weapons can require police involvement. The number shown by the source is New Zealand-specific; published copy tells readers elsewhere to use their local emergency service.

## Editorial safeguarding floor

**Do not ask a teen to investigate, confront, physically intervene or gather more proof while exposed.** This is an OWR editorial safeguarding floor. It is **not claimed to be an effect or rule established by the cited bullying guidance**. Its purpose is to keep a minor from being assigned the adult safety role.

The related bystander direction—to get adult or emergency help rather than watch, cheer, film or physically intervene when unsafe—is also safety-first editorial guidance, not evidence that one response prevents harm.

## Immediate safety boundary

Immediate danger, assault in progress, a credible weapon threat, serious injury, or trapping bypasses CLEAR OUT. Get to safety where possible and contact local emergency help now. Do not delay to complete the framework.

No extra unsupported safety branches have been added. The article does not introduce stalking, abuse or self-harm pathways. It does not give self-defence or legal advice.

## Claims intentionally avoided

- No guarantee that leaving, reporting or a firm line prevents harm or retaliation.
- No claim that every insult, disagreement or fight is bullying.
- No mind-reading, diagnosis, shame or claim that a teen must stay perfectly calm.
- No retaliation, crowd performance, threats, leaked material or public exposure campaign.
- No instruction for a parent or teen to confront the other young person or family.
- No amateur investigation, physical intervention or evidence gathering while exposed.
- No claim that institutional reporting always produces a fast or fair outcome.
- No implication that repair for the teen’s own action erases the harm done to them.
- No presentation of *The War Playbook* as safety guidance; the optional bridge is restrained and the article stands alone.

## Editorial notes

Research is kept in a compact source note so the parent guide can lead with ordinary scenes, scripts and scannable actions. CLEAR OUT remains in the accepted exact order. Parent authority over safeguarding and follow-up is preserved while ordinary wording and choices increasingly return to the teenager.
