# Sources and Claim Audit

**Research date:** 2026-08-20  
**Status:** `DRAFT_ONLY` — not approved, built, published, scheduled, or distributed.

## Sources

1. **Education Endowment Foundation, “Collaborative learning approaches”**  
   https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/collaborative-learning-approaches  
   **Claim-safe use:** Search result and the EEF overview describe collaborative learning as more than placing pupils together; structured approaches and well-designed tasks matter. The article uses this only to support a request for clear roles and a defined first task. Direct page fetch was blocked by HTTP 403; no quantitative EEF claim is used.

2. **Edutopia, “Group Work That Works” (2019)**  
   https://www.edutopia.org/article/group-work-works/  
   **Claim-safe use:** Directly inspected 2026-08-20. Describes educator practices including small groups, clear norms, and meaningful interdependent roles such as summarizer, questioner, and clarifier; its discussion notes that defined roles can provide structure for students less comfortable with complex social dynamics. Used as classroom-practice context, not proof of an intervention outcome.

3. **Edutopia, “Encouraging Shy Students to Speak Up in Class” (2022)**  
   https://www.edutopia.org/article/encouraging-shy-students-speak-class/  
   **Claim-safe use:** Directly inspected 2026-08-20. Describes alternatives to unsolicited speaking, advance notice before calling on a student, partner participation, and roles in small-group work. Used to support asking for a manageable participation structure, not to diagnose quietness or promise increased confidence.

4. **National Institute of Mental Health, “Children and Mental Health: Is This Just a Stage?”**  
   https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/children-and-mental-health  
   **Claim-safe use:** Supports the boundary to seek help when behaviour/emotions persist, cause distress, or interfere with functioning, and to seek immediate help for unsafe behaviour or talk of harm. No country-specific crisis service is named.

5. **National Institute of Mental Health, “Social Anxiety Disorder: More Than Just Shyness”**  
   https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/social-anxiety-disorder-more-than-just-shyness  
   **Claim-safe use:** Supports the distinction between being reserved and persistent fear of scrutiny/judgment that interferes with school, relationships, or daily life. Used only to reinforce the non-diagnostic support boundary; parents are not asked to diagnose social anxiety.

6. **Liu et al., “The relationship between social anxiety and peer relationships among adolescents: A systematic review and meta-analysis” (2020)**  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7758784/  
   **Claim-safe use:** Its prospective-study synthesis supports avoiding a simple causal story between peer experiences and adolescent social anxiety. Used only for the editorial guardrail that one difficult group-work day does not establish peer rejection, exclusion, or a disorder. It does not test the article’s parent scripts.

7. **Clauss & Blackford, “Behavioral inhibition and risk for developing social anxiety disorder” (2012 review)**  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7954867/  
   **Claim-safe use:** Supports the guardrail that inhibited/quiet behaviour is not deterministically a disorder. No prevalence figure is used because the review primarily concerns earlier development rather than 12–17-year-old classroom group work.

## Claim boundaries

| Article claim | Support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Group work can be easier to enter with a clear role or defined first task. | EEF overview; Edutopia classroom-practice examples | Does not promise participation, inclusion, grades, or friendship. |
| A quiet teen may benefit from a small work-focused entry line. | OWR editorial synthesis | Not a clinical social-skills intervention; teen choice remains central. |
| Parents can request classroom structure without requesting rescue. | Edutopia examples plus OWR synthesis | Does not require a teacher to disclose other students’ information or arrange friendship. |
| Persistent distress or impairment warrants closer support; immediate danger bypasses ordinary advice. | NIMH | Group work is not treated as a diagnosis or substitute for urgent help. |

## Portfolio distinctness

- `quiet-teen-team-sport-belonging`: teammate participation and coach contact in sport; this article is the classroom group-formation moment.
- `teen-wont-talk-after-school`: home-arrival parent timing; this article is a teen-owned school contribution plan.
- `quiet-or-lonely-teen`: preferred solitude versus unwanted disconnection; this article does not decide whether the teen is lonely.
- `help-teen-start-conversation-without-making-it-weird`: peer conversation broadly; this article limits itself to entering, contributing to, and recovering within one academic group task.

## Claims intentionally avoided

- Quietness is not called social anxiety, laziness, exclusion, or a deficit.
- No script is claimed to make classmates welcoming or to guarantee group success.
- No advice tells a parent to contact classmates, arrange friendship, or take over the assignment.
- The Social Playbook is an optional, teen-owned soft bridge only after the practical answer.
