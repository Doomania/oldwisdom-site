When Your Teen Is Doing More Than Their Share of a Group Project

Use a Contribution Ledger to separate agreed work from assumptions, help your teen make one neutral request, and use the school route proportionately when a live group task is at risk.

By Eric Han · 24 September 2026 · 7 min read

For busy parents of teens 10–16: when your teen says, “I’m doing all the work,” you do not have to decide who is right, email the teacher in anger, or tell your teen to carry the whole project. Start with facts.

Use a Contribution Ledger: write down the agreed work, what can actually be seen so far, the current gap, your teen’s own next contribution, one neutral request, and the point when an adult needs to step in.

This is an OWR editorial tool, not a tested conflict-resolution programme. It cannot prove who is lazy, make classmates cooperate, or guarantee fair marks. Its job is to help a teen raise a specific school-work problem without turning it into a verdict about another person.

Start with a smaller question

A group project can feel unfair quickly. A deadline is close, messages go unanswered, and one student may seem to be doing more. “They do not care” may be true, partly true, or wrong. It is not yet useful evidence.

Try this parent response:

“I can hear that this feels unfair. Before we decide what anyone meant, let’s write down what the group agreed, what has been done, and what still needs doing.”

Ask for the work facts: task, roles, deadline, shared document or message thread, and visible contributions. Clear roles and visible status checks are classroom-practice supports for group work; they do not guarantee equal effort or fair marks. Edutopia’s discussion is context for keeping the conversation tied to the work.

Make a Contribution Ledger

Open a note and fill in six short lines together.

  1. Agreed task and roles — What is the project? Who said they would do which part? What is due, and when?
  2. Visible contributions — What is in the shared file, slides, research list, messages, rehearsal, or other agreed place? Use dates where they help.
  3. Current gap — What specific piece is missing, late, unclear, or at risk?
  4. My teen’s next contribution — What will your teen complete or share next, and by when?
  5. One neutral request — What does the group need from whom, by what time?
  6. Escalation threshold — What would make a teacher check appropriate: no reply after a clear request, a missed agreed point, a deadline that puts the work at risk, or a school process that says to report earlier?

For example: Mia was to gather two sources for slide 3 by Wednesday; Jay was to make slides; your teen was to write the conclusion. Jay’s outline and your teen’s notes are visible, but the sources are not. Your teen adds their conclusion by lunchtime and sends: “Mia, can you add the two sources for slide 3 or tell us by 7 pm if we need a different split?” If there is no reply or source, the group sends the teacher a short factual update before class.

The ledger is not a scorecard of worth. Do not list guesses such as “Mia is lazy” or “Jay never cares.” A missed contribution can have many explanations. The useful fact is whether agreed work is visible and whether the group can complete the task.

Help your teen make one clear request

Where your teen can do it safely and reasonably, let them make the first request. A parent can help practise it, but should not write as if they are the student or contact classmates on the teen’s behalf.

A usable teen message is:

“Hi everyone. We agreed that I would do the conclusion, Jay would make the slides, and Mia would find sources for slide 3. I’ve added my draft. Mia, can you add the sources by 7 pm, or let us know if we need to re-split the work? We need to finish before class tomorrow.”

If the task split was never clear, try:

“We have the project due Friday and still need research, slides, and a conclusion. Can we agree who is doing each part by tonight, then put it in the shared file?”

Keep the request short and about the next action. Do not add old complaints, copy a parent into the chat, or threaten a teacher report. Rude or unsafe contact belongs on the teacher route.

What a parent can say without taking over

Your role is to steady the conversation and help your teen prepare a usable next move:

“I won’t decide what your classmate meant. I can help you make the request specific, check the school process, and practise what you want to say.”

“Your part is to do the work you agreed to and make one clear request. My part is to step in with the school if access, formal support, wellbeing, or safety needs an adult.”

Do not do their section, monitor classmates, or build a case from rumours. A teacher update is about the live task, not punishing a peer.

When to involve the teacher

If the neutral request does not resolve a clear risk to the work, use the school’s normal route. Follow the teacher’s stated process if one exists.

A teen or group message could say:

“Hello [Teacher]. We are working on [project], due [date]. Our agreed roles are [brief list]. We have completed [visible work], but [specific piece] is still missing after our message on [date/time]. We are continuing our own parts. Could you advise the next step for the group?”

A parent message, if adult communication is needed, can stay equally factual:

“My teen has shared the agreed roles, visible work, and one unanswered request about the live [project]. We are not asking you to decide anyone’s character. Could you let us know the appropriate school process for a contribution issue that may affect the deadline?”

This seeks guidance and a fair process, not a promise that work or marks will be redistributed.

Choose the right lane

Ordinary live task

Use the ledger and teen-led request where possible. Keep the issue inside the current task: agreed work, visible contribution, current gap, and next step.

Access or formal support

If an inaccessible tool, language barrier, missing adjustment, or another access issue is affecting contribution, this is not simply a peer problem. A parent may lead established school or support communication and keep the teen involved where possible.

Persistent distress or interference

If group conflict is causing distress that lasts, is getting worse, or is interfering with school, home, sleep, friendships, or day-to-day life, widen the support. Speak with the appropriate school wellbeing or health professional rather than repeatedly refining the ledger. The National Institute of Mental Health describes persistent distress and interference as reasons to seek professional help; its crisis contacts are US-specific, so use local services elsewhere.

Urgent safety

Threats, harassment, coercion, or unsafe behaviour are school-safety matters: use the school’s established safeguarding/report route rather than asking a teen to manage danger through a group chat. Talk of self-harm or harming someone else, or immediate danger, needs urgent local emergency or crisis help. NIMH advises urgent help when someone is in immediate danger or talks about harming themselves or others.

The conversation for tonight

“This may be unfair, and we do not have to decide why tonight. Let’s make a short ledger: what was agreed, what is visible, what is missing, and what you will do next. Then you can send one neutral request. If the work is still at risk after that, we will use the teacher’s process with the facts. If access, wellbeing, or safety is involved, adults will take the right route.”

That is the full job: facts before frustration, one clear request, then proportionate support.
