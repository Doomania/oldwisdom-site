Your Teen Doesn’t Need a Confidence Speech Before a Presentation—They Need Reps

Use three short preparation reps—one run-through, one likely question, and one reset line—without taking over your teen’s event.

By Eric Han · 24 September 2026 · 6 min read

Your teen has a presentation, audition, interview, tryout, or difficult meeting coming up. They are pacing, saying they will be terrible, or insisting they do not want to talk about it.

The instinct is understandable: You’ll be fine. Just be confident. You know this.

But “be confident” is not something a teenager can put in their bag and use tomorrow. It also turns a practical preparation problem into a verdict on how they feel.

Try a smaller job: offer three reps, then stop. One out-loud run-through. One likely question. One reset line. Your teen chooses the material, decides whether they want the practice, and still owns the event.

This is not a way to guarantee a grade, a place on a team, a job, or a calm performance. It is simply a short way to make tomorrow feel less unprepared.

Start with permission, not a pep talk

Do not open with a review of everything they should have done earlier. Ask what kind of help is wanted.

“You’ve got the presentation tomorrow. Do you want an audience for one run-through, one question, or no help from me?”

“I can listen for five minutes. After that, it is yours again.”

“Would it help to choose one bit that feels least ready, or would you rather leave it tonight?”

If the answer is “no,” accept it unless there is an ordinary responsibility you still need to hold, such as getting materials ready or arriving on time. You can keep that boundary without turning practice into a forced performance.

Avoid trying to settle the result in advance:

  • “You’ll definitely get picked.”
  • “There is nothing to worry about.”
  • “Just fake confidence.”
  • “You have to practise until it is perfect.”

You cannot promise any of those things. You can offer one manageable preparation choice.

Use the Three-Reps Plan

The point is not to rehearse every possible outcome. It is to give your teen three small chances to meet the actual moment before it arrives.

1. One out-loud run-through

Ask them to choose the opening, the key answer, or the section that is hardest to begin.

For a school presentation, that might be the first 30 seconds. For an audition, it might be the first transition. For an interview, it might be their answer to “Tell me about yourself.” For a tryout, it may be describing why they want the role.

Say:

“Pick the bit you most want to say once out loud. I’ll just be the audience.”

Do not interrupt to improve every sentence. When they finish, use one factual question:

“Was there a word you got stuck on?”

“Do you want to keep that opening, shorten it, or try it once more?”

One run-through is enough. If they ask for another, do another. If they do not, leave the work with them.

2. One likely-question rehearsal

Government interview guidance recommends thinking about what an interviewer may ask and practising answers with someone you trust. That does not make a parent a hiring expert. It gives you a useful, limited role: ask one ordinary question and let your teen answer in their own words.

Try:

“What is the question you most hope nobody asks?”

“Want me to ask it once, then you can decide whether the answer needs changing?”

For a history presentation, it could be: “Why does this source matter?” For a school captain interview: “What would you change?” For a drama audition: “What do you think this character wants?”

Do not write a polished adult answer for them. Help them find the point they mean, then let the language sound like a teenager.

3. One reset line

A reset line is not a magic sentence. It is something to use if their mind goes blank, they lose their place, or a question is unclear.

Choose one that fits the setting:

“Could you repeat that question, please?”

“Give me a second—I want to say this clearly.”

“I’m going to check my notes for the next point.”

“Could I come back to that after the next question?”

For an interview, asking for a question to be repeated or explained is an ordinary option. For a classroom presentation, a teacher may have a different routine. Your teen can choose a line that feels natural and allowed in that setting.

Keep your feedback small enough to use

After the three reps, do not give a score out of ten. Give one observation and one choice.

“Your first point was clear. Do you want to leave it there or make one small change?”

“I could hear the main idea. Is there one sentence you want shorter?”

“You handled that question. Want another, or are you done?”

The job is not to persuade your teen that they now feel confident. It is to help them notice what is prepared and what still needs a decision.

If they freeze halfway through, do not rush in with the missing words. Wait. Then ask:

“Do you want the next prompt, or do you want to restart that sentence?”

That keeps the practice theirs.

When a parent should do more than practise

This guide is for an ordinary upcoming performance moment. It is not a reason to ignore agreed support.

If your teen has an accommodation, a communication need, an injury, a disability-related support plan, or a school arrangement that affects the event, use that plan. A parent may need to contact the relevant teacher, coordinator, or support person rather than making the teen manage formal access alone.

Also look beyond rehearsal if fear or distress lasts for weeks, disrupts school, home, friendships, or daily life, or leads to persistent avoidance. A health professional, school support person, or qualified mental-health professional can help assess what is going on. If your teen talks about harming themselves or someone else, or there is immediate danger, seek urgent local help rather than treating it as a presentation problem.

Try this tonight

  1. Ask whether they want an audience, one question, or space.
  2. Let them choose one section to say out loud.
  3. Ask one likely question.
  4. Choose one reset line.
  5. Give one observation, then stop.

Your teen does not need you to declare that they are ready. They need a chance to prepare in a way that still belongs to them.
