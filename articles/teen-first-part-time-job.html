Your Teen’s First Part-Time Job: How to Help Them Start Without Hovering

Use a short first-job routine to help your teen ask normal questions, check basic safety information, and own routine employer communication without taking over.

By Eric Han · 1 September 2026 · 6 min read

A first part-time job can make a household busy with forms, shift times, rides, uniforms, and manager messages. It can make a parent want to take the wheel.

A better role is smaller and more useful. Help your teen prepare, check the ordinary basics, and then let them handle normal communication with the employer. That gives them room to take the job seriously without asking them to figure out unfamiliar workplace systems alone.

Use this sequence: SIGN → SCAN → STEP BACK. It is not a safety guarantee, legal advice, or a test of whether your teen is “ready.” It is a short household routine for the moment an offer or first shift becomes real.

A teen can be capable of the work and still not know what to ask before the first shift. Be a sounding board, not their manager.

SIGN: let your teen name the job and the questions

Before anyone signs routine paperwork or replies to a manager, ask your teen to explain the offer in their own words.

  • What is the job, and when is your first shift?
  • What do you already know about the tasks?
  • Who will train you or supervise you at the start?
  • What are you unsure about?
  • What do you need from me: a second pair of eyes, a ride plan, or help thinking through a question?

Do not quiz them for a perfect answer. They may want help interpreting a form or planning transport, while still wanting to send the employer message themselves. Treat those as separate things.

If they need to ask a routine question, help them make it clear and brief, then let them send it:

Hi [Manager’s name], I’m looking forward to starting on [day]. Could you confirm who I should report to when I arrive and whether I need to bring or wear anything specific? Thank you, [Name]

That is normal job communication. The teen can own it.

SCAN: check the ordinary basics together

A scan does not transfer workplace safety responsibility to the family. Employers remain responsible for a safe workplace. It helps a teen notice what they need to know and a parent distinguish an ordinary question from a concern that should not be brushed aside.

Training and supervision

Ask whether the teen knows how they will be trained and who they can go to during a shift. New workers need clear instruction and supervision, especially when the workplace, tasks, or equipment are unfamiliar.

Useful questions include: “Who is showing me how to do this?” “Who should I ask for if I am not sure?” and “What should I do if the person training me is not available?”

Tasks and equipment

Ask your teen to describe the tasks they expect to do, including equipment, cleaning products, lifting, food preparation, vehicles, or anything else that is new to them. They do not need to know every detail before day one. They do need to know that stopping to ask for instruction is normal.

A simple sentence can help: “I have not been shown how to use this yet. Who can train me?”

Hours, transport, and the household plan

Confirm how the teen will get there and home, what happens if a shift runs late, and how they will tell you about a schedule change. This is household coordination, not a reason to take over the employer relationship. If a manager changes a routine shift, the teen can reply, then update you about transport or household impact.

Injury, emergency, and local rights information

Ask whether the teen knows what to do if they are hurt, feel unwell, see an immediate hazard, or need help during a shift. They should know whom to tell at work and how to contact you if needed.

Also find the official local labour or child-employment authority that applies where you live. Use that source for questions about permitted work, pay, breaks, hours, permits, and reporting options. Those details vary by place, so do not rely on a generic social-media post or another family’s experience.

STEP BACK: keep normal employer communication with your teen

After the scan, let your teen read routine messages, ask ordinary questions, confirm availability, and follow up about a first shift. You can still review a draft, talk through a confusing request, or help with household logistics. An employer needs to learn how to contact the person doing the job, and your teen needs a chance to practise responding clearly.

Stay involved when there is an immediate safety concern, a situation that seems illegal or exploitative, an emergency, or a problem your teen cannot safely handle alone. Parents also keep their responsibilities around transport, safeguarding, and the household. Stepping back from routine communication is not stepping back from care.

A five-minute first-job script

Use this at the kitchen table, in the car, or whenever the offer arrives:

  1. Teen: “Here is the job, my first shift, and what I understand so far.”
  2. Teen: “Here are the questions I want to ask.”
  3. Parent and teen: Check training, supervisor, tasks and equipment, hours, transport, injury or emergency steps, and the local rights source.
  4. Teen: Sends routine questions or replies to the manager.
  5. Parent: “I am here if you want to think something through or if something feels unsafe.”

The first few shifts may bring tiredness, new routines, and small mistakes. Instead of immediately fixing each problem, ask: “What happened?” “What do you think you need to do next?” “Is this a normal question for your supervisor?” or “Do you want help drafting the message, or do you just want to talk it through?”

If your family uses OWR resources, The War Playbook can support preparation under pressure, while The Social Playbook can support asking clear questions and listening. Neither replaces workplace training, supervision, or local official guidance.

Keep the sequence simple: SIGN → SCAN → STEP BACK. Help your teen understand the start, check the basics together, and leave normal employer contact in their hands.

Sources and limits

This is general guidance, not legal advice, a safety protocol, or a promise of safety or job success. Employers retain responsibility for workplace safety. Parents should remain involved in immediate danger, illegal or exploitative conditions, emergencies, transport, and household safeguarding. Use the relevant local official authority for rules and reporting options.

  • Mortimer (2010), The Benefits and Risks of Adolescent Employment: adolescent employment is context-dependent; this review does not show that a particular job or parent conversation causes a result.
  • International Labour Organization, Young Workers: young workers can face higher non-fatal occupational-injury rates; this does not mean every teen job is unsafe.
  • CDC/NIOSH, Keeping Teens Safe and Healthy at Work: supports attention to training, supervision, hazards, hours, injuries, emergencies, and rights; it is U.S.-specific guidance.
  • UK HSE, Young people at work: supports the general need for clear instruction, training, and supervision for young people new to work; no UK-specific rules are stated here.
