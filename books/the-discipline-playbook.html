THE WISDOM BEHIND THE PRACTICE

Classical wisdom alongside practical habit research

The Sources and Notes identify Marcus Aurelius's Meditations, Epictetus's Discourses and Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography, alongside modern research. The classical passages are identified as the author's renderings. Chapter 1 turns the question of acting now into a concrete commitment.

Eric Han adapts the ideas through contemporary situations and practical exercises. The excerpt below is his writing from the manuscript.

Chapter 1 — The Promise Nobody Heard

Tane makes the deal at 6:38, standing at the dishwasher.

Plate in. Straight upstairs. Ninety minutes. Done by eight.

He says it in his head with real conviction, the way you'd say it to a teacher who was actually listening. Nobody heard it. That's the thing about this kind of promise. There's never anyone in the room.

[…]

A promise with no shape isn't a plan. It's a mood you had at 6:38.

[…]

One next action. Not the whole thing. The first move, small enough to start without a run-up.

A rough when. Tonight after dinner. On the bus. Saturday morning.

A QUESTION PARENTS ASK

What does Stoicism have to do with my teen's habits?

Here it means looking at what your teen can choose next, not calling them lazy when a plan slips; The Discipline Playbook turns that idea into one small first action and a rough time to try it.

The book draws on Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus alongside practical habit research; it does not claim an ancient quote proves a modern method. In the public Chapter 1 excerpt, “The Promise Nobody Heard,” Tane makes a detailed plan only in his head while standing at the dishwasher. The selected passages then turn to naming one first action and a rough time; the full chapter's exercise also asks who or what will notice. A missed day can invite a different plan, not a character judgment. A parent can offer the excerpt, while the teen decides whether to use it; adjust expectations for access, workload or additional support needs.
